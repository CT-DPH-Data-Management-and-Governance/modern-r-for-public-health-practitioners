7  Describing and Testing

Packages covered in this part: dplyr, janitor

You built a line-list in the last chapter — one row per case, with an age, a set of dates, and outcome flags. Now we get to ask questions of it. This chapter is the everyday inferential toolkit: describe what’s in the data, cross-tabulate one variable against another, and run the handful of statistical tests that answer “is this pattern real, or could it be chance?”

If you’re coming from SAS, this is PROC FREQ and PROC MEANS territory — just tidier, and with the whole thing scriptable end to end.

WarningThis analysis runs on fake data

Everything below runs on the synthetic line-list from the previous chapter. The town names and case counts are real, but every age, sex, date, and outcome was randomly generated. The shape of the patterns is realistic — deaths really do skew older — but no row is a real person. Never report these numbers as real, and never let one of these tables walk out the door as if it described actual patients.

7.1 Getting set up

We’ll lean on two packages: dplyr for the summarising and janitor for the epi-friendly tables. In your own covid-briefing/ project you’d start by reading the file you built:

linelist <- read_csv(here("data", "synthetic-covid-linelist.csv"))

Then we recreate the age_group column from the previous chapter — most of the questions in this chapter are about how outcomes vary across ages, and a handful of ordered bands is far easier to tabulate than raw years:

library(dplyr)
library(janitor)

linelist <- linelist |>
  mutate(
    age_group = case_when(
      age < 18 ~ "0-17",
      age < 50 ~ "18-49",
      age < 65 ~ "50-64",
      age < 80 ~ "65-79",
      TRUE     ~ "80+"
    )
  )

7.2 Section 1 — Describing what you have

Before any test, look at the data. The first question about almost any outcome is simply how often did it happen? — counts and percents.

janitor::tabyl() is the tool for that. Hand it a column and it returns a tidy table of counts and proportions; adorn_pct_formatting() turns the raw proportions into readable percentages. If you’ve reached for PROC FREQ in SAS, this is the same job.

linelist |>
  tabyl(died) |>
  adorn_pct_formatting()
 died     n percent
   No 34316   97.4%
  Yes   906    2.6%

That’s your case fatality snapshot: about 2.6% of cases in this line-list died. tabyl() is a data frame like any other, so it drops straight into a report or a gt table later without any wrestling.

Counts answer “how many.” For a continuous variable like age you want the shape — where the middle sits and how far it spreads. summarise() gives you whichever statistics you name, and the classic epi move is to compute them split by outcome so you can compare. Here’s “does age differ between people who died and people who survived?”:

linelist |>
  group_by(died) |>
  summarise(
    n = n(),
    mean_age   = round(mean(age), 1),
    median_age = median(age),
    min_age    = min(age),
    max_age    = max(age)
  )
# A tibble: 2 × 6
  died      n mean_age median_age min_age max_age
  <chr> <int>    <dbl>      <dbl>   <dbl>   <dbl>
1 No    34316     41.4         41       0     100
2 Yes     906     74.8         79       0     100

Read across the two rows and the story jumps out. Among the people who died, the mean age is about 74.8 and the median 79; among survivors the mean is 41.4 and the median 41. Deaths skew old — decades older, on average. The mean and median sitting close together within each group also tells you neither is being dragged around by a few extreme outliers.

This one table is often the whole descriptive section of a briefing. But it also raises the obvious follow-up: we can see that deaths are older, but is the association between age and death strong enough to trust? That’s what the next section is for.

7.3 Section 2 — Cross-tabulation with a statistical test

The natural next step is a two-way table: age group down the side, outcome across the top. tabyl() takes two variables and builds exactly that, and the adorn_* helpers layer on the pieces you want to see.

linelist |>
  tabyl(age_group, died) |>
  adorn_totals("row") |>
  adorn_percentages("row") |>
  adorn_pct_formatting() |>
  adorn_ns()
 age_group             No         Yes
      0-17 99.9%  (4,598)  0.1%   (4)
     18-49 99.5% (17,542)  0.5%  (85)
     50-64 98.8%  (7,491)  1.2%  (88)
     65-79 92.8%  (3,604)  7.2% (281)
       80+ 70.7%  (1,081) 29.3% (448)
     Total 97.4% (34,316)  2.6% (906)

There’s a lot packed into that pipe, so here’s what each adornment does:

  • adorn_totals("row") adds a totals row across the bottom.
  • adorn_percentages("row") converts each cell to a row percentage — so each age group’s numbers add to 100% across its own row.
  • adorn_pct_formatting() formats those as percents.
  • adorn_ns() puts the raw counts back in, in parentheses, so you never lose sight of how many people each percentage rests on.

Why row percents? Because the row is the age group, so the percentage in the Yes column is the case fatality rate within that age group — exactly the number an epidemiologist wants. Reading down that Yes column, the CFR climbs steadily: roughly 0.1% for 0–17, 0.5% for 18–49, 1.2% for 50–64, 7.2% for 65–79, and 29.3% for the 80+ group. Nearly three in ten of the oldest cases died. That’s the association from Section 1, now resolved group by group.

Seeing a trend in a table and establishing it statistically are two different things. A chi-square test asks whether the association between age group and outcome could plausibly have arisen by chance if the two were actually unrelated.

age_died <- table(linelist$age_group, linelist$died)
chisq.test(age_died)

    Pearson's Chi-squared test

data:  age_died
X-squared = 5176.2, df = 4, p-value < 2.2e-16

table() builds the plain contingency table (chi-square wants counts, not percentages), and chisq.test() does the rest. The null hypothesis is that there is no association — that outcome doesn’t depend on age group. The test returns a huge X-squared statistic (around 5176) on 4 degrees of freedom and a p-value below 2.2e-16, which is R’s way of printing “smaller than I bother to show.” In plain terms: if age and death were truly unrelated, a table this lopsided would essentially never happen. We reject the null.

WarningA tiny p-value is not a big effect

With this many cases, almost any association will come back “significant” — the p-value is partly a measure of your sample size. A huge n makes the test exquisitely sensitive, so it will flag even trivial differences. Don’t let a row of zeros after the decimal point fool you into thinking you’ve found something important. The effect size — the actual jump from 0.1% to 29.3% CFR you read off the cross-tab — is what makes this finding matter. The p-value only tells you it’s not noise; the percentages tell you it’s a big deal.

7.3.1 When cells are small: Fisher’s exact test

Chi-square relies on an approximation that gets shaky when the expected count in any cell is small — the usual rule of thumb is that no expected cell should drop below 5. That happens all the time when you slice down to a single small town or a rare outcome. Fisher’s exact test computes the exact probability instead of approximating it, so it stays honest on sparse tables.

Let’s force the situation by looking at one small town, Hartland:

hartland <- linelist |> filter(town == "Hartland")

table(hartland$hospitalized, hartland$died)
     
      No Yes
  No  56   0
  Yes  1   2
fisher.test(table(hartland$hospitalized, hartland$died))

    Fisher's Exact Test for Count Data

data:  table(hartland$hospitalized, hartland$died)
p-value = 0.001753
alternative hypothesis: true odds ratio is not equal to 1
95 percent confidence interval:
 4.415925      Inf
sample estimates:
odds ratio 
       Inf

Hartland has only 59 cases, and the table() shows why chi-square would struggle: the cells are thin and at least one is a flat zero. fisher.test() handles that without complaint and returns a p-value of about 0.0018 — strong evidence of an association between being hospitalized and dying, even in this tiny slice.

The teaching point: reach for Fisher when the counts are small. If you run chisq.test() on a sparse table it’ll warn you that the approximation may be unreliable — that warning is your cue to switch. On small or zero-heavy tables, Fisher is the trustworthy choice.

7.4 Section 3 — Testing a trend in proportions

The stakeholder didn’t just ask “is there an association” — they asked about trends in proportions, like an outcome rising over time or across ordered categories. A plain chi-square treats the age groups as unordered labels; it would give the same answer if you shuffled them. But our age bands have a natural order, and the interesting claim is specifically that CFR rises monotonically from young to old.

The test for trend in proportions (a Cochran–Armitage-style test) is built for that. It needs two things in order: the number of deaths in each group, and the number of cases in each group. We build those with a grouped summarise(), then arrange() to lock in the order:

cfr_by_age <-
  linelist |>
  group_by(age_group) |>
  summarise(deaths = sum(died == "Yes"), n = n()) |>
  arrange(age_group)

cfr_by_age
# A tibble: 5 × 3
  age_group deaths     n
  <chr>      <int> <int>
1 0-17           4  4602
2 18-49         85 17627
3 50-64         88  7579
4 65-79        281  3885
5 80+          448  1529
prop.trend.test(cfr_by_age$deaths, cfr_by_age$n)

    Chi-squared Test for Trend in Proportions

data:  cfr_by_age$deaths out of cfr_by_age$n ,
 using scores: 1 2 3 4 5
X-squared = 2918.6, df = 1, p-value < 2.2e-16

sum(died == "Yes") counts the deaths in each group (a TRUE/FALSE vector sums to the number of TRUEs), and n() counts everyone. prop.trend.test() takes the deaths and the group sizes and asks: is there a directional trend in the proportion across these ordered groups?

The result — X-squared around 2919 on 1 degree of freedom, p below 2.2e-16 — says yes, emphatically: CFR increases in a clear linear fashion across the age bands. Because this test uses the ordering of the groups, it’s a sharper instrument than a plain chi-square when the order is meaningful. It’s asking a more specific question (“does it trend?”) and so gives stronger evidence when the answer is yes.

The same machinery answers the stakeholder’s “outcome by year” question directly: group by year instead of age band, feed the yearly death counts and case counts into prop.trend.test() in chronological order, and you’re testing whether the outcome trended up or down over time. Any ordered grouping — age bands, years, weeks of a wave — works the same way.

7.5 Wrap-up

You can now do the three things that make up the bread-and-butter of case-level analysis: describe a line-list with counts and summaries, cross-tabulate two variables and test the association with chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, and test a trend across ordered groups. That’s the everyday inferential toolkit, and it’ll carry you through most routine surveillance questions.

What it won’t do is untangle variables that move together. Older cases might also be more likely to be hospitalized, or to belong to a particular group — and these simple tests look at one relationship at a time. The next chapter, Modeling, is how you get adjusted effect estimates that hold age, sex, and the rest constant all at once.
